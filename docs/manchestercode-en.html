In one breath

Manchester encoding = an electrical-signal rule that always flips in the middle of every bit, so the receiver can recover the clock from the signal alone.

Manchester encoding is a line-coding scheme that forces a signal transition at the midpoint of every bit period. The direction of the transition (rising or falling) encodes 0 or 1, and that transition lets the receiver recover timing without a separate clock signal (self-clocking). It was used in 10BASE-T Ethernet and similar links, but needs roughly twice the bandwidth of simpler encodings to carry the same bit rate, since a transition is required every single bit.

When grown-ups say it

Manchester Encoding
A signal rule that flips exactly once at the middle of every bit. A line-coding scheme built for self-clocking.
Self-Clocking
Recovering the beat from the signal alone. No separate clock wire is needed.
NRZ (Non-Return-to-Zero)
A simpler scheme where the signal flips only when the value changes. Repeated values make the boundaries hard to count.
No DC Bias
The signal spends equal time high and low. Keeps charge from building up one-sided on the wire.
Line Coding
The general category of rules turning digital values into signal shapes. Manchester encoding is one member of this family.
Bandwidth
How much signal a line can carry at once. Manchester encoding spends twice the bandwidth for the same bit rate.
10BASE-T Ethernet
An early Ethernet standard that used Manchester encoding. Modern high-speed Ethernet uses different encodings.
8b/10b Encoding
An encoding used more often in today's high-speed links. Keeps the self-clocking benefit while using less bandwidth.
