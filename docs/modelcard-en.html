In one breath

A model card = the label a guest reads before ordering. It states this dish's intended use, limits, ingredient summary, and known issues.

A model card is a document that publishes a model's intended use, out-of-scope uses, a summary of its training data, and its performance and limitations. Where an AIBOM records the provenance of what went into the model for people inside the kitchen — engineers, auditors — a model card is the usage guide for the people who will actually use the model: developers and end users. Both come from the same release, but they serve different readers and purposes.

When grown-ups say it

Model card
The dish's label. A document the guest reads before ordering, stating what it's for and when not to use it.
Intended use
Who this dish is meant for. The first line on the label.
Out-of-scope use
When not to order it. Places this dish doesn't belong, like medical or legal advice.
vs. the ingredient certificate
The certificate is for the kitchen; the label is for the guest. Its companion from the same release. → the ingredient origin certificate
Open-weight model
A recipe anyone can pick up and use. The more public the recipe, the more the label matters. → the parrot world's open weights
Evaluation of limits
Checking ahead of time where this dish tends to go wrong. The numbers on the label come from this kind of testing. → the parrot world's evaluation
Red-team check
The inspector takes one more look before it's posted. The "known issues" line often comes straight from red-teaming. → the security world's red team
Transparency
Posting it instead of hiding it. Having a label at all is where transparency begins.
